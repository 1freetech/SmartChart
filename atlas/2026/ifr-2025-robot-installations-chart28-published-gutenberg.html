Electronics factories installed 142,235 industrial robots worldwide in 2025, again surpassing automotive manufacturers, which installed 138,429. The International Federation of Robotics reported a record 603,307 global factory installations, up 11% from 2024. Its World Robotics 2026 findings, released September 24, reveal how electronics, automotive manufacturing and metalworking share the expanding automation market.

Electronics first reclaimed the industry's leading customer position in 2024 and retained it during 2025. The annual difference reached 3,806 newly installed robots. The figures describe conventional industrial automation across production lines; they are not the number of new humanoid robots, nor a count of factory workers replaced by machines.

SmartChart #28: A record year divided by industry

Electronics accounted for 23.58% of installations, automotive for 22.94% and metal and machinery for 18.00%. Metal and machinery also reached a record 108,571 units, up 22% year over year. Subtracting the three named industries from IFR's global total leaves 214,072 robots, or 35.48%, across all remaining industry classifications. The residual is calculated rather than separately reported as one industry.

A donut accurately represents these four mutually exclusive shares of the same 603,307-unit annual total. Their percentages add to 100% before display rounding. IFR also counted 5,079,078 industrial robots operating worldwide at the end of 2025, a different stock measure. Adding that installed base to annual installations would double count machinery.

China still dominates deployment

The regional breakdown highlights a separate dimension. Asia installed 457,315 industrial robots in 2025, Europe 73,054 and the Americas 57,044, with the balance elsewhere. China alone accounted for 354,226 installations, nearly 59% of the worldwide count, while the United States recorded 38,428 and overtook Japan for second place. Geographic totals cannot be appended as additional slices to the customer-industry donut because each robot already belongs to an industry.

The electronics lead reflects a wider market for industrial equipment, including high-speed handling, assembly, machine tending and inspection. Actual production gains depend on machine reliability, commissioning, workflow design, maintenance, guarding, energy use and staff skills. Neither the number of installed robots nor a supplier shipment implies independently measured productivity improvement.

IFR forecasts annual installations of approximately 655,000 in 2026 and 806,000 by 2029. Those numbers are projections, not completed deployments. Its executive summary also distinguishes proven machine vision and predictive maintenance from general-purpose physical AI and humanoids, which are not yet broadly industry-grade.
