Linux reached 3.95% of operating systems recorded in Valve's October 2026 Steam Hardware and Software Survey, up from 3.05% in September. Windows remained dominant at 93.70%, while macOS accounted for 2.35%. The latest figures put Linux just below one in 25 systems in the voluntary monthly survey and highlight the growing visibility of SteamOS and alternative desktop Linux distributions in PC gaming.

Valve collects anonymous hardware and software information from users who choose to participate. The results describe the surveyed Steam population, not every active Steam account, every gaming PC or the worldwide desktop market. They cannot establish a count of Linux players without a published sample size, and changes between months may reflect survey composition as well as changes in installed operating systems.

SmartChart #30: The operating systems behind Steam gaming

A waffle chart makes a fixed 100% whole tangible. September's reported shares were 95.03% Windows, 3.05% Linux and 1.92% macOS. October recorded 93.70%, 3.95% and 2.35%, respectively. Rounding each month to 100 equal cells produces 95 Windows, three Linux and two macOS cells for September, against 94 Windows, four Linux and two macOS cells for October. The exact Linux increase was 0.90 percentage points, not a full point.

The difference is meaningful as a survey observation but cannot prove Linux gained precisely 0.90% of all Steam customers. Windows fell 1.33 percentage points, and macOS gained 0.43 percentage points. The three changes reconcile to zero, preserving a consistent whole. Because macOS grew without gaining a rounded square, original decimal shares accompany the illustration.

CachyOS, SteamOS and compatibility software

Valve's October survey lists CachyOS at 0.65% of all surveyed systems, SteamOS Holo at 0.55%, Arch Linux at 0.35%, Linux Mint 22.3 at 0.33%, and a separately labeled SteamOS category at 0.32%. Those are shares of the combined survey, not percentages of Linux systems alone. The SteamOS labels should not be silently merged without Valve's definitions or device-level information.

Valve's Proton compatibility layer allows many Windows games to run on Linux, but anti-cheat restrictions, graphics drivers, launchers and device support can still prevent particular titles from working. SteamOS on ARM and Valve's ARM hardware strategy extend the engineering story, while FEX's x86 translation fixes illustrate remaining software challenges. openSUSE's desktop changes offer another view of broader Linux development.

The October result is a useful signal for developers evaluating Linux support, but the overwhelming majority of Steam survey systems still run Windows. Repeated measurements, stable sampling and real game-compatibility testing matter more than projecting a single month's percentage into a permanent trend.
